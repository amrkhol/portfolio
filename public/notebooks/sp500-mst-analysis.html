Which stocks hold the market together?

A minimum spanning tree over S&P 500 return correlations reduces 124,251 pairwise numbers to 498 edges. The stocks at the hubs and the stocks out on the branches become two portfolios — then both are held, untouched, through a window the tree never saw.

01

The result, first

Over the 46 trading days after the tree was built, the peripheral book returned +10.20% against the index's +3.53%. The central book returned +2.18% — it trailed.

That ordering is what the literature predicts, and it is also almost meaningless at this sample size. Forty-six days of three series cannot separate a real effect from the spread you would get by drawing fifteen tickers out of a hat. Section 06 is the honest version of this paragraph.

02

The tree

Every stock is connected to every other by a correlation. Keeping all of them tells you nothing — 124,251 numbers is not a picture. The minimum spanning tree keeps the n−1 edges that connect all 499 names through their tightest correlations and discards the rest.

What survives is a backbone. Illinois Tool Works sits at the centre with twelve neighbours — an industrial conglomerate selling into construction, autos and electronics is about as close to a proxy for the cyclical economy as a single ticker gets. The central book that falls out of the ranking is ten parts Industrials, Financials and Real Estate.

The periphery is the opposite kind of business. Six of the fifteen are exchanges and data vendors — CBOE, CME, ICE, VRSK, EFX, JKHY — companies that charge fees on activity rather than owning the outcome of it. They hang off the tree on a single edge each.

03

Method

  1. Returns

    Weekly log returns, r = log Pt − log Pt−1. Weekly rather than daily: 130 observations is enough to estimate correlation structure over two and a half years, and carries far less microstructure noise. Tickers below 80% coverage, and any with zero variance, are dropped — both produce NaN correlations, and a spanning tree cannot rank a NaN edge weight. 499 of 503 survive.

  2. Distance

    The Mantegna transform, d = √(2(1 − ρ)), turns correlation into a metric: perfectly correlated names sit at distance 0, uncorrelated at √2, perfectly anti-correlated at 2. The argument is clipped at zero so floating point cannot push it negative and return NaN. Mean pairwise correlation across the window is 0.201.

  3. Tree

    Kruskal's algorithm over the distance matrix. Self-loops from the zero diagonal are removed first — left in, they dominate the edge set, since a distance of zero always wins. The result is checked for connectedness: a forest is not a tree, and quietly returning one would invalidate every path length downstream.

  4. Scoring

    Centrality averages the percentile ranks of degree and betweenness, not the raw values. On a tree this size betweenness runs up to 0.76 while degree tops out near 0.11, so averaging the raw numbers is betweenness wearing a disguise. Peripherality averages the ranks of three hop distances: from the highest-degree hub, from the most-correlated hub, and the mean distance to every other node.

  5. Test

    Top 15 of each ranking, restricted to names with a complete price series over the test window, then held with no rebalancing. The benchmark is ^GSPC downloaded separately — slicing the constituent matrix gives you 500 stocks, which is not the same thing as the S&P 500.

04

Structure worth looking at

Ordered alphabetically, a 499 × 499 correlation matrix is visual noise. Grouped into GICS sector blocks, the thing the tree is built on becomes visible directly: Energy and Utilities are dense squares, Financials and Real Estate almost as tight, Health Care conspicuously loose.

The unit diagonal is masked out. Left in, it pins the colour scale to ±1 and flattens every real correlation into the pale middle — the most common way a correlation heatmap ends up saying nothing.

Rebuilding the tree separately on each calendar year shows the market loosening and tightening. 2025 is the tight year — average shortest path drops to 11.8 hops and mean correlation rises to 0.261, which is what stress looks like in this representation: everything moving together, so everything is close.

The 2026 column looks like the opposite, but it is half a year of 25 observations. A sparser tree on less data is the expected artefact, not a finding — which is the sort of thing worth saying out loud rather than plotting as a trend line.

06

What this doesn't show

Every one of these is a reason the headline number is softer than it looks. They are listed here rather than in a footnote because deciding which of them matters is most of the work.

  • Forty-six days is not a sample

    Two months of daily observations on three series. A 6.7-point gap over this span sits comfortably inside the distribution you would get by picking fifteen S&P 500 tickers at random. Nothing here is statistically separable from that.

  • Survivorship bias, and it runs one way

    Index membership is scraped as of today, so anything delisted or removed since January 2024 is absent and recent additions are backfilled with history they did not have as members. The bias flatters both portfolios. Wikipedia's second table lists additions and removals with dates; walking it backwards would reconstruct the true point-in-time universe.

  • The benchmark is not like-for-like

    Both books are equal-weighted; the S&P 500 is cap-weighted. Part of any gap is simply the equal-weight effect, which has nothing to do with network position. RSP, the equal-weight index ETF, is the comparison that would isolate the actual claim.

  • Gross returns

    No transaction costs, no slippage, no taxes, no borrow.

  • One window, one start date

    This is a single draw. Rebuilding the tree every quarter on a trailing year, reselecting both books and chaining the out-of-sample periods turns one observation into a time series — the difference between an anecdote and a finding, and the extension worth doing before anything else.

  • The stronger claim is about risk, not return

    What the network literature actually argues for peripheral stocks is diversification benefit, not outperformance. The peripheral book here ran at 21.2% annualised volatility against the index's 11.3% — nearly double. Comparing realised portfolio risk is more likely to show something real than comparing two months of returns.
